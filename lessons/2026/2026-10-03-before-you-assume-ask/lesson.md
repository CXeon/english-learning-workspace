# Before You Assume, Ask

## 原文

Imagine that a teammate sends you a short message: “Please revise the summary before this afternoon.” You could immediately open the document and start changing sentences. But what does “revise” mean here? Does the summary need to be shorter, clearer, or more persuasive? Does “this afternoon” mean a draft at two o’clock or a final version before the end of the day? If you silently choose an answer, you may work quickly in the wrong direction.

A clear follow-up question is not a sign that you were not listening. It shows that you understood part of the request and noticed what was still missing. A useful question often begins with the shared context: “I understand that the goal is to help new readers understand the proposal. Which section should I focus on first?” This is easier to answer than a broad question such as “What do you mean?”

It also helps to narrow the question down. Do not send a long list of every possible doubt. Ask about the one decision that will unlock the next step. If the audience is unclear, ask, “Is this summary for the project team or for people who have not seen the project before?” If the deadline is unclear, ask, “Do you need a draft by two, or should the final version be ready by then?” Specific choices make the hidden difference visible.

After you receive an answer, check your understanding. You might say, “So I’ll shorten the opening, keep the data table, and send you a draft by two. Is that right?” You do not need to repeat the whole conversation. State the important choices and give the other person a chance to correct them while changes are still easy.

Tone matters as well. “Why didn’t you explain the deadline?” sounds like blame. “Could you clarify what needs to be ready by two?” keeps attention on the work. When useful, briefly explain the reason for your question: “To make sure I focus on the right readers, who is the main audience?” This makes your purpose clear without a long apology.

Good collaboration does not require asking more questions all the time. It requires asking at the moment when an assumption could send the work in the wrong direction. A twenty-second question can prevent an hour of rework. Instead of pretending that everything is clear, make the uncertainty visible and turn it into one answerable decision.
