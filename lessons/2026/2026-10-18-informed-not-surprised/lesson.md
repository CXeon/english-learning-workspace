# Informed, Not Surprised

## 原文

Imagine you are halfway through a one-week task when you discover a problem: a file you need from another team will arrive two days late. Nobody has asked you for an update. It is tempting to stay quiet, work faster, and hope the delay disappears before anyone notices. Many people choose silence because they want to look in control, or because they hope to fix everything first.

Hidden problems do not stop growing; they simply grow quietly. When the bad news finally arrives on Friday, it arrives with no time left to react. The person waiting for your work is usually not angry about the delay itself. They are frustrated because the delay removed their choices. If they had known on Tuesday, they could have asked another teammate to help, warned their own manager, or negotiated a smaller scope. On Friday afternoon, none of these options remain. The goal of good communication is simple: no surprises.

A better habit is to keep people informed before they ask. This does not mean writing long reports or scheduling extra meetings. A quick status update is enough: what is done, what is next, and what has changed. Two honest sentences sent early are worth more than a perfect explanation sent too late.

Do not wait for certainty to flag a delay early. You might write: "The data may arrive late; if it does, I will need two extra days. I will confirm tomorrow morning." Flagging a risk is not the same as accepting failure. It protects the plan while there is still time to adjust it. A delay shared on Tuesday is still a plan; the same delay shared on Friday is a failure.

Finally, close the loop. When someone answers your question or helps with your blocker, tell them what happened next: "The data arrived, the report is ready — thank you for checking." Open loops make people ask again and again; a closed loop tells everyone the issue is truly handled. Small updates, sent early and finished clearly, are how steady teammates build trust.
